# Latitude: an editorial philosophy

*An internal guide for whoever writes the next story. It may be you in a year, a collaborator, or a model working from these pages. Read it before you choose anything.*

---

## 1. What Latitude is

Latitude is a collection of encounters with the range of human life. Some are true and some are invented. Each is short enough to finish on a bus and built so that something of it stays.

It is not a course, and it does not claim to improve anyone. It works more like a well-stocked imagination: a store of incidents, people, mechanisms and images the reader did not have before. Months later, one of them is there when something happens. That a stranger's face looks different after you have seen what they were looking at. That a woman paid by the hour noticed the ruler for the universe in a footnote. That a city spent twenty years politely co-writing a madman. The reader is never told what to do with these things, and that is the point.

The governing image comes from the founding brief. Imagine looking back at a life from a century later, as a biographer would. Ordinary afternoons, failed experiments and unremarkable conversations then look like chapters. The people we read about lived their lives forwards, inside ordinary uncertainty, with no idea which afternoons would matter. Latitude tries to put that uncertainty back. It does not promise the reader a remarkable ending. It shows, again and again, that nobody knows how their own story ends, and that this is the interesting part.

## 2. The sensibility

Latitude should be recognisable with the title removed. These are the habits that make it so.

- **Curious before clever.** It is interested in how things actually work: people, institutions, cells, cities, stars. A strange true detail beats a clever line.
- **Attentive to the wrong details.** It notices the thirty cents an hour, the three carriages, the shares in a dead gold mine. Significance often hides in the detail a summary would cut.
- **Comfortable with contradiction.** The same man fed half the world and gassed a trench. Latitude puts both on the page and does not average them into a lesson.
- **Funny when it is funny.** Humour is a way of seeing, not decoration. Bureaucracies, emperors and the human body are all absurd, and saying so is part of telling the truth.
- **Tender without being soft.** It cares about its people, including the difficult ones, and does not comfort the reader at their expense.
- **Occasionally dark.** Some stories should be disturbing. Darkness is told plainly and without spectacle. No lingering, no score swelling under the deaths.
- **Larger than the reader.** The world should feel bigger after each story, and more navigable. The reader is a visitor to that world, not its subject.

### What Latitude never does

- **Flatter.** No "you too could…", no "imagine what you're capable of".
- **Promise a destiny.** Retrospective significance is shown as something biographers *construct*, and often get wrong. It is not a guarantee waiting for the reader.
- **Moralise.** No closing lesson, no "the takeaway", no "remember that…". If the last line would work on a motivational poster, cut it.
- **Profile the reader.** No quizzes about who you are. No inferring insecurities. No content shaped around a presumed weakness. The collection is aimed at a curious adult, not at a diagnosis.
- **Manufacture progress.** No streaks, scores, levels or completion badges. Remembering which doors you have opened is the most the app will ever track.
- **Retell the anecdote canon.** No Edison's thousand failures, Einstein failing maths, the starfish thrower, the Chinese word for crisis, or the frog in boiling water. These stories are tired, and most of them are false.

## 3. How stories work here

This section explains what to do and why. None of it is a template.

### The incident, not the theme
A story is something that happened, to someone, somewhere, on a day. "Credit and recognition" is a theme. "Her sentence went out under the director's signature, and its first line says it was prepared by Miss Leavitt" is an incident. Start from incidents. If you notice you have started from a theme, go and find the incident that carries it, or drop the theme.

### One image at the centre
Every story worth telling here has an image someone could carry away: a speck labelled *N* and then crossed out and relabelled *VAR!*, a policeman saluting a bankrupt, the same face three times. Find the image first. A story without one belongs in the Reading Room.

### Dramatic irony is for restoring uncertainty
Readers usually know how historical stories end. Use that, but in the right direction. The point is not "look, she became important". The point is that she did not know. Put the reader back on the near side of the event: the Tuesday, the uncrossed-out *N*. Let them feel how ordinary it was before the hindsight arrives.

### Words and pictures do different jobs
This is the lesson of the best picture books. When the text says "she found them by the hundred", the picture should show something the words did not say, such as the ledger and the cramp of the columns. Images that only repeat the text waste a page. Counterpoint between the two is where adult sophistication comes in: the picture can contradict, undercut or extend the words.

### The page turn is the unit of suspense
In a picture book the turn is a held breath. Plan the text so each page ends just before something, and the next page delivers it. Put reveals after turns, not in the middle of a page. Use beats, lines that arrive on a tap, for timing within a page. Use them sparingly: they are a drumroll, and a drumroll on every line is noise.

### Little text, large type
A page holds roughly one thought: one to three sentences, set big. The checker enforces a ceiling of about fifty words per beat. If a passage needs more, it needs more pages, not smaller type. Depth comes from selection and sequence, not density.

### Trust the reader; leave the iceberg under water
Leave out what the reader can supply. Don't explain the joke, the irony or the significance. If a fact is devastating, state it in the plainest sentence available and move on. The restraint is what makes it land.

### Ambiguity is earned, not manufactured
When something is genuinely unknown (why Clara Immerwahr died, whether the Kuleshov effect replicates, whether Norton believed it), say so on the surface of the story, briefly and without anxiety. Never invent ambiguity about things that are known, and never resolve real disagreements to make a tidier story.

### Endings open outward
End on an image, a fact or a quiet turn, not a conclusion. The best endings make the reader look at something ordinary differently, such as the far side of the Moon, a bus timetable, or a stranger's face, without being told to. Good endings in this collection change what the *next* ordinary moment looks like.

### Perspective is a choice
Decide where the camera stands: close, distant, the city's view, the editor's, the reader's. Second person ("you") is a strong spice. Use it for a page, rarely for a story, and never to tell the reader what they feel.

### Difficult people are people
Don't write hagiographies and don't make villains. Admirable people did petty things. Terrible things were done by people who also loved their children and fed the world. Let the reader hold both. Don't adjudicate.

### Humour comes from precision
Norton abolished Congress, and Congress continued. The joke is in the exactness of the record. Don't add a wink.

## 4. Choosing what to tell

Use these questions. A strong candidate passes most of them, and an outstanding one surprises you on several.

1. **Is there an incident?** A day, an object, a sentence, a decision, not just a topic.
2. **Is there an image?** Can you see the central page?
3. **Is it unexpected?** Obscure, forgotten, peculiar, or a familiar story from an angle nobody uses.
4. **Would it survive being told aloud** at a dinner table, without slides?
5. **Does it change how something ordinary looks** afterwards?
6. **Is it honest?** Can the documented core be verified, and can the uncertain parts be labelled without killing it?
7. **Does it fit a phone?** Roughly eight to twenty-five page turns, or an interaction that works under a thumb.
8. **Does it widen the collection?** See the range test below.

### The range test
A collection is curated like an album, not a syllabus. Before adding a story, place it on these axes against what is already there:

| Axis | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | a glance between strangers | the expansion of the universe |
| Mode | documented history | pure invention |
| Tone | comic, absurd | tragic, disturbing |
| Era and place | not just Europe and America; not just the last two centuries |
| Protagonist | admirable | compromised, ordinary, anonymous, non-human |
| Form | a few illustrated pages | a full simulation |
| What it leaves behind | a mechanism, a worldview, an image, a question, a laugh |

If a new candidate lands in the same cell as an existing story, it needs to be extraordinary. Watch out for the gravitational pull toward embarrassment, courage and "small things matter". Those were the first instincts, and they are already represented.

### Sources of the unexpected
Look in local histories, court records, obituaries, footnotes in the papers of famous people, the people *next to* the famous person, failed inventions, institutional archives, letters to editors, ethnographies, the history of measurement, the history of mistakes, and fiction's minor characters. A good question to ask is: *who was in the room but isn't in the story?*

## 5. Truth and invention

Latitude publishes history and fiction side by side, so the line between them must always be findable.

**The four labels.** Every factual claim in a historical story is one of the following:

- **Documented.** It is in primary sources or a strong secondary consensus. It can be told as fact.
- **Reconstructed.** It is a plausible staging of documented events, such as a desk, a light or the order of a morning. It is allowed in pictures and framing, and must be listed under "made up" behind the curtain.
- **Interpreted.** It is a view historians hold and others dispute. It must be marked as a view on the page ("some say…").
- **Invented.** It is fiction. It is free in fiction. In a historical story it is allowed only when it is visible as invention.

**Rules for real people:**

- Never invent dialogue for a real person and present it as said.
- Never narrate a real person's private thoughts as fact. You may show what they did.
- Quotations must trace to a primary source or a reliable secondary one. Misattributed quotes are a plague, and this collection does not spread them. If the source can't be confirmed, paraphrase and say "it is said".
- Where reliable sources disagree on a date or detail, choose the safer phrasing on the page and record the disagreement behind the curtain.
- Statistics carry their source and their uncertainty, and are phrased no more strongly than the source.

**Behind the curtain** every story lists what is real, what is made up, what is still open, and its sources. The curtain is one tap away and never interrupts the story. Fiction stories still have a curtain: it says plainly that the story is invented, and names whatever real things it leans on.

**Fiction is free.** A speculative premise does not need scientific validation to be philosophically worthwhile. What fiction owes the reader is internal truth: people who behave like people, and consequences that follow.

## 6. Medium

The story chooses the medium.

- **Illustrated book** (`js/book.js`): pages, beats, glosses, notes, lenses, choices, small interactions. This is the default, because it is cheap to make well and easy to keep.
- **Book plus a hook.** A page with a modest custom interaction (`on`), such as blinking two plates, making a choice, or toggling editions.
- **A room.** A full custom encounter, like the Dance Floor, only when the *interaction is the argument*: when doing the thing teaches what no page could.

Climb this ladder only as far as the story requires. An interaction that decorates rather than means should be cut. A test: if you removed the interaction and replaced it with a sentence, would anything be lost?

## 7. Visual language

- Each story has its own palette and drawing style, set in its stylesheet, and must work in both light and dark modes.
- All stories share a grammar: large display type (Fraunces), small mono captions, paper grain, generous margins, one thought per page.
- Illustrations are inline SVG built from flat shapes, the economy of mid-century print and picture books. They must be legible at 375px wide. Detail goes where the eye should go.
- Every illustration has alt text that describes what matters, not every shape.
- Motion is meaning or nothing. Page turns can be leafs, cuts, rises, fades or drifts, chosen per page. With reduced motion, everything becomes a quiet fade.

## 8. Voice and style

- British spelling (*humour, harbour, colour*), because the corridor started that way.
- Short declaratives, varied by the occasional long sentence that earns its length.
- Concrete nouns, exact numbers, real names.
- Banned words and moves: *journey, unlock, empower, mindset, the power of, lessons, takeaway, inspire*, rhetorical question chains, "imagine if…" openings, exclamation marks (other than in quotations, like Hubble's).
- Dates are stated plainly ("22 April 1915"). Places are named.
- Titles should intrigue, not summarise. A phrase from the story is often best.

## 9. Building a collection

A collection has five stories, give or take. Sequence it the way an album is sequenced: open with something that hooks, vary tempo and tone, put the darkest piece where it has room to breathe, and end on something that sends the reader back out into the world. Each collection gets a placard in the corridor and a name, usually a phrase from one of its stories.

## 10. The review before publishing

Read the whole story on a phone and answer honestly:

- **The read-aloud test.** Does every page sound right spoken? Does any sentence explain what the picture already showed?
- **The cover-the-picture test.** Do the words still carry the story? Now cover the words: do the pictures add something?
- **The poster test.** Could any line be printed on a motivational poster? Rewrite it.
- **The stranger test.** Would someone with no interest in self-improvement enjoy this for its own sake?
- **The curtain test.** Is everything presented as fact documented, and is everything uncertain labelled?
- **The second-reading test.** Is there something you notice only the second time?
- **The range test.** Does it widen the collection?

## 11. On breaking these rules

These are habits of taste, not laws. A story may break any of them if it does so deliberately, and if the break is what makes it work. Write a sentence in the story's file explaining which rule it breaks and why. Consistency of sensibility matters. Uniformity of structure does not.
